What each one actually is

One principle sits under the first three: when a computer asks another computer to do something, it gets an answer back saying whether it worked. All three items are places where nobody opens the answer.

01

"Saved" when nothing was saved

Done

What you'd see

You finish a review quiz and get every question right. The screen celebrates, and the milestone card moves on your board. Next morning the card is back where it was, as if you never took the quiz — and taking it again costs money, because the quiz is written fresh by Claude each time.

Proving mastery out loud has the same hole, plus a second one: if your connection drops at that exact moment, the screen sits on a spinner that never finishes and offers no way out.

Why it happens

The browser asks the server to record the pass. The server replies — "recorded", or "your login expired", or "something broke". The code never opens that reply. It moves straight to the celebration.

So a refusal and a success are literally indistinguishable from where you're sitting. The most common real cause is mundane: your login quietly expires while you're working, and every save after that is politely declined.

What I'll change

Read the reply before celebrating. If it didn't work, stay on the screen, say what happened in one sentence, and put the button back. Nothing about the passing case changes — you'd never notice the fix unless something failed.

What landed

All four saves now read the reply. The decision about what a refused save means was pulled out into one small file of its own, so it is written once and tested directly rather than being repeated in four screens — the project's own rule for anything with real branching.

That file sorts a refusal into six kinds and decides two things for each: the sentence a learner reads, and whether pressing the button again could actually work. Being signed out gets no retry button, because a retry that can't succeed is the same lie in a different costume.

Each screen then got the failure it deserved. The quiz keeps "Quiz Passed!" and your score on screen — you did pass, and that stays true — and only swaps the "saving…" line for what went wrong plus a way out. Mastery returns you to your score with the same. The board slides the card back where it was and says so, instead of leaving it in a column the database never agreed to.

The stranded spinner, found on the way

The mastery screen had a second fault I hadn't separated out: if your connection dropped at the moment you confirmed, the code fell out of the handler entirely and left the screen spinning on "Saving your mastery…" with no button anywhere. Not a wrong answer — no answer, forever. Same fix covers it.

How to check me

14 new tests cover the decision itself, including the one this item exists for: reports an expired session as a failure rather than a success. Whole suite went from 1,296 to 1,310, all passing, with lint, types and a production build clean.

In the app: sign in, leave a quiz open long enough for the session to lapse, then pass it. You should get your score and an honest sentence — not confetti.

02

The milestone panel never says anything went wrong

Done

What you'd see

You open a milestone and your learning diary is blank. You wrote in it yesterday. The entries are safe in the database — the app simply failed to fetch them, and showed you nothing rather than saying so.

Why it happens

The code says, in effect, use whatever came back, or an empty list if nothing did. A failed request produces nothing. An empty diary also produces nothing. They render identically.

This is a rule Hugh already wrote down for itself: "you have nothing" and "we could not load it" are different sentences to the person reading them.

And the other half: buttons that do nothing

Five more places on that same panel have the mirror of the same fault, on the way out rather than the way in. Accepting a suggested correction, editing a diary entry, re-tagging one, adding one, and asking Hugh to write the milestone summary — each sends a change and never checks whether it was taken. When one is refused, the button simply does nothing. No message, no spinner, no change on screen. You would reasonably assume you misclicked.

Two small mercies, confirmed: your typed text is not thrown away when an entry fails to save, and nothing is lost — it just never left. But nothing tells you that.

What I'll change

For the diary load, use the pattern that already exists eight lines below it — the checklist on that same panel handles this correctly, with its own "this failed" state that keeps your ticks visible. Copy that shape rather than invent one.

For the five silent buttons, reuse the decision file built for item 1. It already knows what each kind of refusal means and how to word it; these screens simply weren't asking.

What landed

The diary now has four states instead of two, and the difference between them is the whole item: still loading, failed to load, genuinely empty, and has entries. A failed load says "we couldn't load your learning diary — your entries are safe, this screen just didn't get them", with a Try again button that re-runs the same load rather than asking you to close the card.

That four-way decision went into a small file of its own, tested directly, because three separate sections of the panel were asking the same question and getting it wrong the same way.

Every button that sends a change now reads the reply. When one is refused you get a red notice naming what didn't happen, and the screen puts itself back the way the database actually has it. Your typing is never discarded: a refused new entry leaves your draft in the box, and a refused edit leaves the editor open with your rewrite still in it.

Two things found on the way

The gates were worse than the diary. The Review Quiz and Mastery buttons read the same empty list, so a failed load didn't just show a blank diary — it told you to "add at least one learning diary entry before starting the review quiz" and withheld the button until you did. Being instructed to redo work you have already done is a worse failure than a blank screen. Both gates now say they couldn't check, and offer the retry.

One save was lying twice. Ticking an item on the milestone checklist updates the panel immediately and also tells the board card behind it. Neither was ever checked against the server, so a refused tick stayed ticked in both places until you reloaded the page. It now reverts both and says why.

A third, smaller one: closing the panel refreshes the "3 entries" badge on the card, and a failed refresh was being read as a count of zero — so the badge would vanish. It now keeps the number it had. A stale count is a smaller lie than an invented one, and a card has no room to explain itself.

One deliberate exception

The rule for this run is that failure wording on these screens is ours, because the milestone routes answer in developer shorthand. The summary route turned out to be the exception all the way through: it refuses with "Add a diary entry before generating a summary", "Milestone is not mastered", "Summary is too long". Those are sentences written for a person, and each is more use than our generic line — so those three, and only those three, are shown as the server wrote them.

This is the same rule item 5 rests on, not a contradiction of it: show the server's message where the server wrote one for a human. It is opted into for named cases rather than guessed at, because a refusal is not in general a promise that its wording is fit to read.

How to check me

15 new tests, including the one this item exists for: reads a refused load as failed, not empty. The suite went from 1,310 to 1,325, all passing, with lint, types and a production build clean.

In the app: open a milestone with the server refusing reads. The diary should say it couldn't load and offer Try again — not look empty — and the Review Quiz button should say it couldn't check rather than telling you to write an entry. Then let your session lapse and press Accept, Save, or Generate summary: each should tell you it didn't take.

03

The server doesn't check its own saves

Done

What you'd see

Two things. You tick items off a milestone checklist, they show as ticked, and later they're unticked again. And separately: Hugh writes you a milestone summary — which costs real money each time — and it may never be stored, so it gets written and paid for again the next time you open that card.

Why it happens

Same principle as items 1 and 2, one layer deeper. The server asks the database to save, and the database answers "saved" or "failed". Three places don't look at that answer. The server then tells your browser everything is fine.

The summary case is the worst of the three, because it also writes a success into your own admin dashboard. So the health page reports the feature as working while nothing was stored.

Hugh's own written rules already forbid this, in the "never do this" list. These three slipped past.

What I'll change

Look at the answer. If the save failed, tell the browser it failed instead of claiming success — and don't record a win in the dashboard that didn't happen.

What landed — and the card was wrong about the fix

"Look at the answer" turned out to be necessary and not enough. A database write that matches no rows doesn't report an error — it reports nothing at all, and quietly changes nothing. So a route that only checks for errors still concludes it worked.

That is not a rare case. It is exactly what a permission refusal looks like from the server's side: the row simply isn't visible, so nothing matches, so nothing is written, so nothing is said. The quietest failure in the stack.

The real fix is to make each write hand back the row it changed, and treat "nothing came back" as a failure. That check lives in one small file with its own tests, so no route has to remember the trap.

Your call on the paid-for summary

You chose: when Hugh has written your summary but the database won't keep it, you get the summary anyway. It renders, with a plain line saying it couldn't be saved and won't be there next time, and the Download button beside it still works.

There's also a Try saving again that costs nothing. The words already exist — only the storing failed — so the retry stores what's on screen rather than asking Claude to write it a second time.

Worth recording why this came up: I asked whether the failure could be prevented on the database side instead. It can't. The column is unlimited text, and the summary is capped by the model at roughly 4,000 characters — about five times under the only limit in that file, which doesn't even apply to this path. Size was never the problem. What's left is dropped connections, timeouts and permission refusals, and none of those can be designed away.

Something item 4 had to fix early

The summary route recorded what it spent after storing the document. Adding a failure exit above that line would have skipped the spend record entirely — quietly creating item 4's defect in the very route item 3 was fixing. The cost is now recorded the moment Claude answers, before anything can go wrong, because the money is gone either way.

How to check me

7 new tests on the check itself, including the one this item exists for: a write that matched no rows is reported as a failure, not a success. The suite went from 1,325 to 1,332, all passing, with lint, types and a production build clean.

Being straight about the limit: those tests cover the decision, not the three routes wired to it. This project has no harness for testing a route against a fake database, and this run isn't building one — same approach as items 1 and 2, where the decision is tested and the wiring is verified by reading. If you'd rather I built that harness, say so and it becomes its own item.

04

Money spent that nothing records

Done

What you'd see

Nothing, directly — this one only shows up as a gap in your cost page. Some real spend never appears there at all.

Why it happens

Claude charges the moment it answers, whether or not the answer is any good. Occasionally it returns something malformed that Hugh can't read. In three places the code checks the answer first and only records the cost afterwards — so a malformed answer means money spent and no record of it.

Most of Hugh already gets this right and records the cost first. One route even has a comment explaining exactly why. These three are the exceptions.

What I'll change

Record the cost the moment the answer arrives, before inspecting it. Two of the three are literally moving one block of code above another. The third throws away a running total it has already counted — I'll log it instead.

What landed

Two of the three were exactly as described — one line moved above another, so the cost is written down the moment Claude answers rather than after we've decided whether we liked the answer.

I also checked the claim on this card rather than repeating it. It holds: five other places that pay Claude — mastery scoring, the mastery recap, topic refinement twice over, and the learning summariser — all already record the cost first. One of them explains why in a single line above the code. These three were the exceptions, not the pattern.

The third one was worse than the card said

The document reader doesn't ask Claude once. It asks, and if the answer comes back unreadable it asks again — then gives up. It was carefully adding up what both tries cost, and then throwing that total away on its way out the door. The comment above it even promised it wouldn't.

That's the most expensive call in this item by a distance: your whole uploaded document goes into each attempt, and you're charged for what goes in as well as what comes back. Two of those, invisible.

The fix is a small piece of shared machinery with its own tests, and it exists for one specific reason: the moment of billing and the moment of failure are one line apart. Claude answers — you're charged — and the very next step is the one that can blow up. So the cost is now handed over before that step, and it survives the failure rather than disappearing with it.

One found on the way, from item 3

The fact-checker throws away the answer to its own save, the same way the three routes in item 3 did. So an entry could be marked as fact-checked on screen while the database never recorded it — and since only fact-checked lines may be quoted in a review quiz, a phantom verdict there turns into a quiz question later. Fixed here rather than left for a sixth item.

How to check me

10 new tests on the accounting itself, including the one this item exists for: reports usage from an attempt that billed and then threw, and sums every attempt when all of them fail. The suite went from 1,332 to 1,342, all passing, with lint, types and a production build clean.

Then your cost page. Expect the number to go up — not because we started spending more, but because it was under-reported before. Document uploads that failed are where the difference will show.

05

The real reason gets replaced by a shrug

Done

What you'd see

You hit your daily usage limit in Ask Hugh. The chat replies "Sorry, something went wrong. Please try again." So you try again, and get the same thing. Nothing anywhere tells you you've hit a limit.

Why it happens

The server writes a specific, useful message and sends it back. The chat window throws it away and substitutes a generic one. A deliberate refusal and a genuine crash come out reading the same.

There's a smaller second problem underneath: that error text gets saved as though Hugh said it, so it becomes part of the conversation Claude reads back on your next message.

What I'll change

Show the message the server actually sent, and keep failure text out of the saved conversation.

What landed

Ask Hugh now reads what the server actually said before deciding what to tell you. Hit your monthly limit and you get the sentence written for that — the one naming what to do about it — instead of an apology that leaves you pressing send again.

A refusal also appears as what it is: a notice beside the conversation, with no avatar and no speech bubble, because the one thing it must never look like is Hugh talking. It carries Send it again only when sending again could actually work — a lapsed session and a spent budget get no button, since a retry that cannot succeed is the same lie in a different costume.

Small thing with a real payoff: when the server says how long to wait, you're now told. It has always sent that number and nothing has ever shown it to you.

The second half was bigger than this card said

The card said the error text became part of the conversation Claude reads back. True — and it went three places further. It was written into the exported transcript, folded into the checklist rail's reading of what you'd covered, and handed to the summariser.

That last one is the one that matters. The summary is what you save to your learning diary, and your diary is the only thing a review quiz is allowed to quote. So a sentence Hugh never said could travel all the way to being the thing a quiz asks you about.

The fix isn't care, it's shape: a failure now carries no reply at all, so there is nothing to accidentally add to the conversation.

And the worst one, found on the way

When the summariser itself failed, the words "Unable to generate summary. Please try again." were handed to the summary panel as the session's story — looking exactly like a real one, with a working Save button underneath it. One click filed that sentence in your learning diary.

The panel is now told explicitly when there is nothing to show, and there is no save button on a failure because there is no summary to save.

The judgement call, resolved

Item 1's card flagged that these two items look contradictory: there I use our wording, here the server's. They aren't in conflict. The rule is to show the server's message where the server wrote one for a person — and here it did, for exactly two refusals, which are named one by one in the code. Everything else on this route still answers in shorthand written for logs, and that is still kept away from you.

How to check me

19 new tests, including the two this item exists for: shows the budget refusal instead of a generic apology, and carries no reply on any failure, so a refusal cannot become a message. The suite went from 1,342 to 1,361, all passing, with lint, types and a production build clean.

In the app: run yourself out of budget and read what Ask Hugh tells you. Then export the transcript — the refusal should not be in it.
